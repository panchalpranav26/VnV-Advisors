Wills & Trusts

Wills and trusts are essential tools for protecting your family, simplifying decisions, and ensuring your wishes are honored. This section explains them in clear, family-friendly language — no legal jargon, no pressure.

What Is a Will?

A will is a legal document that explains how you want your assets, responsibilities, and dependents handled after you pass away. It also identifies who will oversee those decisions.

  • Names beneficiaries for your assets
  • Names guardians for minor children
  • Names an executor to carry out your wishes
  • Reduces family conflict and uncertainty

According to the American Bar Association, having a will is the simplest and most common form of estate planning — yet 60% of Americans do not have one.

What Is a Trust?

A trust is a legal arrangement where you transfer assets into a separate entity managed by a trustee. Trusts offer more control, privacy, and flexibility than a will.

  • Avoids probate (saving time and court costs)
  • Provides privacy — unlike wills, trusts are not public records
  • Allows structured distribution (e.g., kids at certain ages)
  • Enables protection for blended families or special needs dependents

The California Courts list living trusts as one of the most effective ways to simplify estate settlement and reduce legal processes.

Why Wills & Trusts Matter for Families

Estate planning is not only for people with large estates — it's for anyone with children, a home, savings, or loved ones who depend on them.

  • Protects your children and loved ones
  • Avoids confusion at difficult emotional times
  • Ensures assets go where you intend
  • Prepares family members to manage responsibilities
“Estate planning is an act of love — it spares families from stress, conflict, and guesswork.” — American Bar Association

Common Types of Trusts

Here are the most common trust types used by families today:

Revocable Living Trust

Flexible, changeable, and avoids probate. Most common trust for families.

Irrevocable Trust

Cannot be altered easily; often used for tax or asset protection purposes.

Testamentary Trust

Created through a will; activates after death to protect children or dependents.

Special Needs Trust

Protects eligibility for government benefits while providing long-term support.

What to Consider When Planning

  • Do you want to avoid probate?
  • Do you have minor children?
  • Do you own a home or investment property?
  • Do you need privacy?
  • Do you want structured distribution (certain ages, milestones)?
  • Do you need to protect someone with special needs?

These questions shape whether a will, a trust, or both are best for your family.

Common Questions

Do I need both a will and a trust?

Most families benefit from having both. A will names guardians and covers smaller assets. A trust handles major assets, avoids probate, and increases flexibility.

Is a trust only for wealthy families?

No. Many middle-income families use trusts to avoid probate and protect children.

Do I need a lawyer?

Simple wills can sometimes be created using state resources. Trusts usually require professional guidance. We do not give legal advice — we provide education and help families understand their options.
